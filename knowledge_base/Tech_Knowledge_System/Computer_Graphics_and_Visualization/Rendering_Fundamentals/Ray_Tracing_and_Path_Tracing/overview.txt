[CHUNK: DEFINITION]
Ray tracing simulates light paths from the camera into a scene, calculating interactions with objects to determine pixel color. Path tracing extends this by simulating multiple light bounces, including indirect illumination, for highly realistic images. Both are physically based rendering techniques.

[CHUNK: CORE_CONCEPTS]
- Ray: A vector representing a light path from a source or camera.
- Intersection: The point where a ray hits an object in the scene.
- Global Illumination: Accounting for direct and indirect light contributions.
- Monte Carlo Integration: Statistical method used in path tracing for sampling light paths.
- Bidirectional Path Tracing: Samples paths from both light sources and camera, connecting them.
- Importance Sampling: Directs rays towards important light sources or reflective surfaces.
- Caustics: Light focused by reflective or refractive surfaces.
- Denoiser: Post-processing filter to remove noise from undersampled renders.

[CHUNK: SYSTEM_DESIGN_PERSPECTIVE]
Ray and path tracing systems require efficient spatial data structures (BVH, k-d trees) for rapid ray-object intersection tests. They are often parallelized across GPUs or distributed systems due to high computational demands, especially for complex scenes and high sample counts. Memory management for scene data is critical.

[CHUNK: TOOLS_AND_TECH]
Categorized tools:
- Languages: C++, CUDA, OptiX, OpenCL
- Frameworks: Embree, OptiX SDK, Vulkan Ray Tracing extensions, DirectX Raytracing (DXR)
- Infrastructure: GPUs (NVIDIA RTX, AMD Radeon RX), cloud rendering farms, distributed computing frameworks

[CHUNK: REAL_WORLD_USE]
Used extensively in film and animation for photorealistic visual effects, architectural visualization for accurate lighting simulations, and product design for high-fidelity material rendering. Also applied in scientific visualization for precise light transport analysis and virtual reality for immersive realism.

[CHUNK: FAILURE_MODES]
Common failures include excessive noise from insufficient samples, rendering artifacts from incorrect material properties or light source definitions, and prohibitive render times for complex scenes or high resolutions. Memory exhaustion can occur with large scene geometries or textures, leading to crashes or slowdowns.

[CHUNK: OPTIMIZATION_STRATEGIES]
Techniques include spatial acceleration structures (BVH, k-d trees), importance sampling, Russian roulette for path termination, and denoising algorithms. Progressive rendering provides quick previews, while distributed rendering spreads workload across multiple machines. GPU acceleration is crucial for performance.

[CHUNK: SECURITY_IMPLICATIONS]
While not directly a security domain, rendering engines can be targets for intellectual property theft of scene assets or proprietary algorithms. Remote rendering services could be vulnerable to unauthorized access or denial-of-service attacks. Data integrity of 3D models and textures is important.
